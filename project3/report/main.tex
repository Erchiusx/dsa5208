\begin{abstract}
Parallel chess search creates a conflict between exposing work and avoiding it:
independent subtrees are easy to distribute, but they cannot exploit the same
alpha--beta bounds as a sequential search. We implement serial negamax,
MPI+OpenMP frontier decomposition, a seeded-root alternative, and two OpenMP GPU
offload strategies. On NUS Vanda, six additional positions yield 120 complete
three-trial CPU configuration groups, including 24 strong-scaling points from
72 to 720 allocated cores. At 720 cores, the recorded search-region medians for four longer workloads are
5.57--21.84 times as fast as serial alpha--beta, while two shallow middlegames
remain slower. These are allocation-dependent observations, not population
estimates or end-to-end speedups. A pair of baselines separates scaling of a fixed
task graph from acceleration of the original algorithm. On the initial
position, 112.95-fold fixed-task speedup becomes only 3.04-fold speedup over
serial alpha--beta because decomposition expands search work by 36.3 times.
Seeded-root search reduces wasted work but exposes load imbalance; deeper
splitting helps large allocations while often hurting a 16-core run. GPU
chunking reduces speculative tree construction, yet remains slower than the
paired serial CPU search. Explicit profiling shows that raw data copies account
for only 2.9\% of a depth-eight GPU run: CPU tree generation and the combined
launch/search phase dominate. These observations motivate workload-aware splitting and bound-preserving
scheduling; their benefits require controlled follow-up rather than a universal
preference for more cores.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem, scope, and research questions}
A chess engine chooses a move by considering its own continuations and its
opponent's best replies. A position contains the pieces, side to move, castling
rights, en-passant square and move counters. Legal moves are edges of a game
tree, whose shape changes with checks, captures and terminal outcomes. One
\emph{ply} is one player's move; depth eight is eight plies, not eight full turns.

Our task is to compute a deterministic fixed-depth score and a maximizing legal
root move. This isolates search and scheduling from playing-strength engineering.
Leaves use material values of 100, 320, 330, 500 and 900 for pawn, knight,
bishop, rook and queen. Scores are in centipawns from the root side's perspective.
Checkmate receives a large score adjusted for distance; stalemate and supported
draw conditions receive zero. There is no learned evaluation, transposition
table, quiescence search, iterative deepening or heuristic move ordering.
A reported move is best under this model and depth; it need not be a strong
move according to a competitive chess engine.

The study asks three questions. First, how does frontier depth trade parallel
capacity against lost pruning? Second, does preserving an initial root bound
improve runtime when static MPI assignment remains unbalanced? Third, can GPU
offload repay CPU preparation and device execution costs for this irregular
search? We evaluate multiple positions to identify both useful scaling and
small-workload failure cases. The positions are fixed benchmarks, including
synthetic states, rather than a representative sample of human games.

\section{Algorithm and sources of parallel dependence}
\subsection{From a chess position to a search tree}
The input is one board state, not a dataset of games. At the root, the side to
move chooses a legal move. The opponent then chooses a reply, followed by our
next choice, and so on. These alternating choices form a tree: a node is a
position and an edge is one legal move. The number of children varies because
different positions allow different moves; checkmate, stalemate and draws can
stop a branch early. Appendix~A shows the seven exact input boards alongside
their FEN encodings.

At a fixed depth, the program does not play out every game to its final result.
It scores the reached leaves using the stated material values and chooses a
root move assuming that the opponent will choose the reply least favorable to
us. Thus the first two levels are ``choose the best move, then face the worst
reply''. More plies repeat the same rule. This is a deterministic benchmark of
search and parallel scheduling, rather than an attempt to predict human play.

\subsection{Negamax and alpha--beta pruning}
For legal moves $M(s)$ and the state $s_m$ after move $m$, negamax writes minimax as
\begin{equation}
 V(s,d)=\begin{cases}
 E(s),&d=0\text{ or }s\text{ is terminal},\\
 \max_{m\in M(s)}[-V(s_m,d-1)],&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Terminal rules are applied before the material cutoff. The implementation
negates the evaluation perspective after each move and reverses board changes
when returning. An exhaustive minimax backend disables pruning and provides a
shallow search-control reference.

Alpha--beta carries bounds $(\alpha,\beta)$ and searches a child with
$(-\beta,-\alpha)$. Once $\alpha\geq\beta$, further continuations cannot change
the relevant decision and may be omitted \cite{knuth}. The following small
two-ply example gives the intuition; scores are from the root player's view:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}\toprule
Our candidate move & Opponent replies examined & What we know \\\midrule
A & $+100$, $+200$ & Opponent chooses $+100$ \\
B & $0$, then replies not yet examined & B can yield at most $0$ \\\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
After completing A, the root has a move worth $+100$. The first reply to B
already proves that a minimizing opponent can hold B to $0$ or less, so no
remaining reply to B can make it the better root move. This is a \emph{cutoff}:
the skipped branches have no effect on the best root decision. Searching A and
B independently in parallel may inspect those branches before A's bound is
known. Move order therefore changes work even though it does not change the
exact fixed-depth minimax value.

This dependency makes the algorithm a meaningful parallelization problem.
Within a subtree, recursive choices and cutoffs remain dependent; across
subtrees, a shared search bound could eliminate work. Our basic parallel
implementation deliberately removes some of that dependency to expose tasks.
It must then pay for the resulting additional node visits and merge exact
scores. Running unrelated chess positions on different nodes would not expose
this particular tradeoff.

\subsection{Implementation boundary}
Project C++17 code implements search, evaluation, frontier construction,
MPI/OpenMP scheduling, GPU traversal and instrumentation. The unmodified
MIT-licensed Disservin \code{chess-library}, revision
\code{2a608d58f44776909bb6e2c09505e97ba1451319}, supplies board state, FEN parsing,
legal moves, move application/reversal, draw helpers and UCI formatting
\cite{chesslib}. Its license and provenance are included. All calls to that
host-oriented library execute on the CPU. It does not supply our search or
parallel scheduler. The documented instructor guidance accepted this credited
dependency.

\section{Parallel designs and correctness arguments}
\subsection{Root splitting and deeper frontiers}
The basic hybrid design assigns legal root moves to ranks by task index modulo
rank count. OpenMP uses a dynamic loop within each rank. Every task owns its
board and counters and searches with a full window. MPI combines scores with
\code{MPI\_Allreduce}; unowned scores use a negative-infinity sentinel.
MPI calls occur on the main thread, using \code{MPI\_THREAD\_FUNNELED}.

A root often supplies only tens of tasks. To use hundreds of workers, split
depth $k=2$ or $3$ constructs a larger CPU frontier. Each frontier board is
searched independently for the remaining depth; exact results are combined
bottom-up with the negamax recurrence, including the appropriate sign change
at every ply. Correctness follows by induction: a frontier search returns the
exact value of its remaining subtree, and maximizing negated exact child values
recovers the parent value. Early terminal nodes remain frontier leaves. This
argument relies on full-window task values; merging arbitrary cutoff bounds as
exact scores would be incorrect.

Ranks currently build the same frontier and each scans the task loop, skipping
unassigned indices. MPI ownership is static; OpenMP dynamic scheduling cannot
move an expensive task to another rank. Deeper frontiers increase board-storage
and construction costs, replicated rank-local work, reductions and merge work,
as well as the number of available tasks. The node counter counts frontier
construction once logically, not once per rank; it consequently understates
physical duplicated preparation work at larger rank counts.

\subsection{Seeded-root search}
The \code{mpi-pv} and \code{omp-pv} backends first search the first legal root
move exactly. Its score $a$ is broadcast and used as a fixed lower bound for the
remaining root choices. In the negated child search, the window is
$(-\infty,-a)$. If a child search cuts off, its root-side result cannot improve
$a$; if it proves an improvement, the required value is recovered. Keeping the
exact seed and taking the root maximum thus preserves the root value, although
individual rejected branches may return bounds rather than exact scores.
Stable root ordering resolves equal scores.

This is inspired by the principle of searching a first child before releasing
siblings, as in Young Brothers Wait \cite{feldmann}. Our implementation is a
\emph{root-only fixed-bound variant}; it is not a complete recursive YBWC
scheduler. It neither refreshes $a$ after each improvement nor dynamically
balances work between MPI ranks. These restrictions let us examine the benefits
of retaining pruning separately from scheduling quality.

\subsection{OpenMP GPU offload}
The first GPU design expands the entire legal tree on the CPU, packs flat node
and leaf arrays, then uses \code{target teams distribute parallel for} over
root subtrees. Each device worker performs explicit-stack alpha--beta traversal
and leaf evaluation. Device-side pruning cannot undo CPU work already spent
constructing the full tree.

The chunked design preserves CPU alpha--beta above a fixed chunk depth. When a
chunk is reached, the CPU expands that local tree and sends its child branches
to the device with independent full windows. Exact child results are merged on
the CPU and search continues. This reduces speculative construction but increases
the number of target launches. Chunk depth controls the balance between fewer,
larger transfers/launches and extra generated nodes. Neither backend is a
multi-GPU MPI implementation: MPI+OpenMP CPU search is the principal hybrid
solution, and GPU offload is an additional design comparison.

Both designs preserve the same evaluation and terminal semantics. Actual A40
runs require \code{OMP\_TARGET\_OFFLOAD=MANDATORY} and check
\code{omp\_is\_initial\_device()} to reject silent CPU fallback. Profiling variants
use explicit allocation and \code{omp\_target\_memcpy} to distinguish copies from
target-region execution \cite{openmp}.

claims. New CPU runs used source revision \code{237f06f}; the original scaling
source was \code{efef92e}. Their serial and deeper-frontier implementations are
unchanged. Newer GPU source hashes differ and are recorded separately. The
artifact archive pins the measured sources instead of assuming all results
came from the current repository head.

All corrected launches use \code{--bind-to none}. An earlier controlled
four-thread experiment on the same node took 6.229 seconds under default
one-core binding versus 2.432 seconds unbound, with identical search work.
A separate affinity probe confirmed that the unbound rank remained within the
PBS allocation on the tested node. Open MPI explicitly recommends either no
binding or enough processing elements for threaded processes \cite{mpi}.
The measured affinity correction addresses that particular confinement;
\code{--bind-to none} alone is not a verified placement policy for every run.
It does not optimize NUMA locality;
thread placement and per-rank memory access remain uncontrolled factors.

\subsection{Timing, repetition, and validation}
We report medians of three fresh process invocations in each allocation, without
removing a warmup. Observed ranges show repeat variability, not confidence
intervals. Three trials on one allocation do not quantify variability across
independent allocations. The older initial-position one-worker task baseline
has only one trial; its derived efficiencies carry that limitation.

The program's MPI time is the maximum rank search-region time: frontier
construction, local search, score/counter reductions and merging are included;
process startup and the final timing reduction are excluded. There is no
explicit pre-search MPI barrier in this path; the maximum of rank-local
durations is therefore not a synchronized global start-to-finish measurement. The added Python
runner separately records walltime around the launcher as \code{launch\_seconds}.
GPU total time includes preparation and offload. The original implicit-map
target timer includes copies and synchronization; explicit-copy profiling
separates H2D and D2H, but its target-search region still includes launch and
synchronization rather than pure device arithmetic.

Every complete new CPU group has three distinct successful trials whose scores
match the corresponding serial reference. Shallow tests recorded in the project
compare alpha--beta with exhaustive minimax across seven FENs and depths 1--3,
including checkmate, stalemate, promotion and en passant. Target-host checks
exercise device traversal logic on the CPU; real GPU logs additionally show
\code{ran\_on\_device=1}. Agreement under shared chess rules validates the
search-control paths; it is not an independent proof of all chess rules.
Equal-score legal moves may differ, so score agreement is the automated archive
criterion. It should not be confused with independent verification of each
chosen move's optimal value in every deep run.

\subsection{Two baselines and a work-inflation model}
Let $T_s$ be serial alpha--beta time, $T_{k,1}$ one-worker time for the same
split-depth-$k$ task algorithm and $T_{k,p}$ its time on $p$ allocated cores.
We distinguish
\begin{align}
 S_{\mathrm{alg}}(p)&=T_s/T_{k,p},&
 S_{\mathrm{task}}(p)&=T_{k,1}/T_{k,p},&
 E_{\mathrm{task}}(p)&=S_{\mathrm{task}}(p)/p.\label{eq:metrics}
\end{align}
Define time inflation $H_k=T_{k,1}/T_s$ and node inflation
$A_k=N_{k}/N_s$. They are different quantities: node costs, scheduling,
construction and hardware differences prevent treating node visits as a uniform
unit of time. By definition,
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{S_{\mathrm{alg}}(p)=\frac{pE_{\mathrm{task}}(p)}{H_k}}.
 \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
Thus useful acceleration over serial requires $E_{\mathrm{task}}>H_k/p$.
This identity is an accounting device, not a fitted performance law. It explains
why impressive fixed-task speedup can coexist with modest algorithm speedup.
We additionally use $pT_{k,p}/T_s=p/S_{\mathrm{alg}}$ as a rough allocated
core-time ratio. It is not measured energy or billing cost, and baseline
hardware differences limit causal interpretation.

\section{CPU results: measured gains and limits}
\subsection{Reinterpreting the original initial-position result}
At depth eight, serial alpha--beta visits 25,604,258 nodes in a median
5.34012 seconds. Split depth three visits 929,868,807 nodes: $A_3=36.32$.
Its one-worker time is 198.129 seconds ($H_3=37.10$); the 720-core median is
1.75406 seconds. Consequently $S_{\mathrm{task}}=112.95$ and
$E_{\mathrm{task}}=15.7\%$, but $S_{\mathrm{alg}}=3.04$.
Equation~\ref{eq:identity} explains the difference without selecting a convenient
baseline. An earlier feasibility job had 53 seconds of PBS walltime despite
1.593 seconds of internal search, illustrating the importance of scope.

The 72/144/288/720-core medians were 9.094/3.048/1.945/1.754 seconds. The
72-to-144-core ratio exceeds two, as do some new workload segments. We report
these observations without attributing them to caching or scheduling: rank
assignment changes, NUMA behavior, processor frequency and allocation effects
were not isolated. Strong scaling of an application region does not establish
an end-to-end superlinear algorithmic benefit.

nor uniformly poor scaling describes these workloads. At 720 cores, Kiwipete,
rook/pawn, tactical and minor-piece positions are 10.31, 5.57, 11.84 and

contributor to its higher algorithm speedup, but the observations do not isolate

\subsection{Efficiency and resource cost}
At 720 cores, Kiwipete has 52.6\% fixed-task efficiency and 10.31-fold algorithm
speedup; minor pieces has 20.4\% efficiency but 21.84-fold algorithm speedup.
The ordering by task efficiency differs from the ordering by speedup over
each position's own serial reference; it is not a direct comparison of
absolute usefulness across different chess workloads. Their time-inflation factors are about 36.78 and 6.72,
respectively. Holding a measured parallel time fixed, a larger one-worker reference time in the numerator
raises the reported task speedup by definition. The reference choice itself does not
improve execution time. This arithmetic relation cannot diagnose the cause of
a measured slowdown.

The longer workloads still consume roughly 33--129 times the serial
\emph{allocated search-region core-time} at 720 cores. More cores buy latency,
not automatically economical resource use. For the minor-piece case, going
from 288 to 720 cores reduces median time from 0.647 to 0.483 seconds, while
increasing core-time from about 186 to 348 core-seconds. This gives a concrete
basis for selecting a configuration by either latency or resource budget.
It does not include launcher costs or measure energy.

\subsection{Search time is not launcher-inclusive response time}
The added runner also records elapsed time around each \code{mpirun} invocation.
Using those recorded medians changes the speedup substantially
(Table~\ref{tab:scope}). Both the serial and parallel paths use the MPI executable
and launcher; the serial-wrapper measurement is not a standalone serial program's
response time. Startup, shell/network delays, MPI initialization, output and
other non-search work remain in this measure. The difference from search time
cannot be assigned solely to communication overhead, and the difference between
medians is not a median per-run overhead. For example, Kiwipete's launcher-inclusive
serial median is 19.462 seconds although its serial search median is 5.142 seconds.
The wrapper noise is substantial and cannot be ignored or cleanly subtracted.

\subsection{Preserving pruning does not resolve load imbalance}
The original seeded-root initial-position study visits exactly the same
25,604,258 nodes as serial at depth eight, yet medians on one/two/four
four-thread nodes are 3.010/2.853/4.205 seconds. An instrumented four-node run
attributes only 0.347 seconds to the seed. Afterwards rank zero searches
14.14 million nodes for 3.459 seconds, whereas other ranks search
2.64--3.88 million nodes in 0.333--0.444 seconds. The longest observed rank
search is approximately three times the mean of the four rank work times.
These timers cover local search regions rather than a measured idle-time trace,
but directly expose a severe static-assignment imbalance. The seed alone is
not an adequate explanation of the slow critical path.

In the new rook/pawn workload, seeded root improves from 1.448 seconds on one
four-thread node to 1.029 on four, compared with 3.188 seconds serial. In the
minor-piece workload, it remains near 11 seconds despite more nodes. A good
bound and a balanced schedule are distinct requirements. The initial legal
move is not guaranteed to be a good seed; the fixed bound also misses later
improvements available to serial alpha--beta. We therefore avoid claiming a
universal seeded-root advantage or equating this implementation with a mature
parallel chess engine.

\section{GPU results: what phase timings establish}
\subsection{Full trees versus on-demand chunks}
On an A40 node with NVHPC 24.7 and the GCC 13.2 toolchain, the full-tree
backend returns serial-matching scores at depths three through five. Its
CPU-built tree grows from 9,322 to 5,072,212 nodes; at depth five its initial
pilot total is 1.798 seconds versus 0.007303 seconds for paired serial search.
That pilot establishes correct offload, not useful acceleration.

A later same-allocation comparison repeats each design three times
(Table~\ref{tab:gpu}). Chunk depth three generates more nodes than chunk depth
two, but reduces chunk launches from 844 to 58 and is faster. Thus fewer
generated nodes alone do not minimize GPU runtime; launch granularity also
matters. Relative to full-tree offload, chunk depth three reduces the measured
median by 3.88 times, yet remains about 59.6 times slower than paired serial.
This is a reduction within the measured offload family. It does not establish
a GPU advantage over the CPU reference, nor isolate launch count as its cause: CPU
generated work, transfers and device traversal also change with chunk depth.

At depth eight, one chunk-depth-three pilot takes 154.521 seconds, versus
3.940 seconds for its paired serial search, generating 428.8 million nodes
across 30,619 chunks. Deeper search amortizes one-time setup but exposes the
large cumulative cost of repeated construction and target work. We do not
combine GPU and CPU medians from different compiler paths as if hardware were
the only changed variable.

\subsection{Explicit profiling challenges the transfer-only explanation}
The explicit-copy depth-eight profile takes 154.620 seconds, with 57.525
seconds CPU preparation, 3.563 H2D, 0.853 D2H, 0.243 device probe and 92.313
seconds in target-search regions. Copies total 4.417 seconds, or 2.86\% of
end-to-end time; CPU preparation is 37.20\%, and target launch/search is
59.70\%. Allocation and residual bookkeeping account for the remainder.
The run uploads 33.54 GB cumulatively, but volume alone does not identify the
dominant cost. Reducing that measured copy time to zero while leaving other
phases unchanged would yield only
\begin{equation}
  \frac{154.620}{154.620-4.416629}\approx 1.029
\end{equation}
fold total speedup. This counterfactual holds the recorded non-copy phases fixed. It is not a
bound on a redesigned implementation that also changes computation, overlap or
CPU preparation, and the single profile does not estimate run-to-run uncertainty. Even eliminating the measured
CPU preparation alone would leave roughly 97 seconds, far above the paired
3.689-second serial search in this profiling job.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{gpu_phases.pdf}
\caption{One explicit-copy A40 depth-eight profile, partitioning its measured
154.620-second total. Large segments are labeled with fractions of total;
the legend preserves small phases instead of visually hiding them. ``Other''
includes the device probe, allocation and residual host work. Target time

The correct next target is therefore the search representation and granularity,
not only raw transfer bandwidth. CPU-native move generation forces speculative
preparation, while branch divergence, limited branch-level parallelism and
many launches are plausible contributors to target-region time. These latter
mechanisms were not separately profiled, so the data do not establish their
individual contributions. Profiling has narrowed the design question without
answering it completely. Persistent device data, GPU-native move generation
or larger batched frontiers would change the design and require new validation;
they are future work, not demonstrated optimizations.

\section{Limitations, design lessons, and reproducibility}
\subsection{What the evidence can and cannot support}
First, these are fixed-position strong-scaling experiments. Depth and position
are held fixed within each curve; depths differ across workloads. Increasing
chess depth is not a weak-scaling protocol because branching and pruning change
nonlinearly. We make no weak-scaling claim and do not infer population-wide
chess performance from six selected additional positions.

Second, the source and numerical workload are controlled more tightly than the
physical execution. Jobs use different allocations; the logs do not establish
uniform clocks, NUMA locality or background load. New CPU runs use three
repetitions in one allocation, not three independently randomized allocations.
Displayed ranges are descriptive envelopes, not inferential intervals. Claims of superlinear speedup, small performance
differences or universal strategy superiority would exceed this evidence.

The workload groups also differ in position and depth; duration is not an
independently manipulated factor. The shorter cases cannot establish a causal
work-size threshold. A same-position depth sweep would be required. All full-node
runs fix one rank per node, so this study cannot identify an optimal rank/thread
layout or separately quantify an MPI tax against a standalone OpenMP build.

Third, serial baselines and MPI allocations differ in resource environment.
The one-worker task baseline includes the hybrid path's bookkeeping; node
inflation, time inflation and core-time are complementary measurements, not
interchangeable. Frontier construction is replicated at each rank but counted
once logically. Timing scopes must remain explicit, especially for subsecond
searches and GPU target timers.

Fourth, the evaluation is intentionally limited. Material-only search can
prefer unrealistic quiet moves or fluctuate between odd and even depths.
A correct score under this model does not validate tactical quality. All
implementations share the same move library, so agreement can miss a common
rule or evaluation defect. The archive checks score agreement; stronger
validation would independently evaluate selected best moves and add perft
move-count checks without changing the measured algorithm.

Finally, the first Kiwipete one-worker split-three trial exceeded a 180-second
limit; a later job repeated the same workload successfully three times with an
800-second allowance. An endgame pilot also required retrying. These attempts
are preserved and excluded from successful medians. Retry policy and shallow
fallback workload selection can bias what is easy to run, so the two short
middlegames remain visible rather than disappearing from the report.

\subsection{Recommendations tied to observed evidence}
The measurements motivate testing decomposition as a function of workload and
available workers; they do not validate an automatic selection rule. The 16-core comparisons motivate testing a shallower frontier
before choosing split three. For large allocations, task size estimates or
demand-driven distribution could address the measured rank imbalance, while
bounded batches can control scheduling overhead. A coordinator consumes real
resources and should be included in efficiency accounting.

Bound sharing is the next algorithmic priority: finding a useful child early
and propagating safe bounds can reduce work inflation. It also introduces
synchronization, stale bounds and cutoff cancellation, so a dynamic scheduler
alone is not sufficient. Correctness must distinguish exact scores, lower
bounds and upper bounds, including re-search when necessary. A sensible
experiment would add one mechanism at a time and measure changed node counts,
rank balance and total time under the same inputs.

For reducing the measured search region, the longer workloads show gains at
high core counts; the wrapper results require a more qualified latency claim; for
resource-sensitive use, the incremental time reduction may not justify the
allocated core-time. The two short cases show no full-node advantage in this protocol; identifying
a general size threshold requires a matched workload-size sweep. GPU
redesign should first avoid CPU generation of branches that serial search
would prune, then measure launch and device utilization separately. None of
these proposed mechanisms is claimed as implemented here.

\section{Conclusion and AI-use declaration}
The experiments show that scaling a task graph and accelerating a search
algorithm are different achievements. Deep frontiers fill many cores but
increase the amount of search; a first-child bound preserves pruning but leaves
load balance unresolved. Across the recorded new allocations, 720-core median search time is shorter
than its serial reference on four longer positions and longer on two shallow
ones. This is a descriptive comparison under the stated timing scope. The GPU experiments further show that reducing generated work helps,
yet eliminating raw transfers would barely affect the measured depth-eight
runtime. The causal contributions of cache locality, dynamic imbalance, collectives and
GPU divergence remain unresolved. Useful optimization requires controlled
changes and independent repeated allocations, not an attractive speedup curve alone.
